## Top Developments

### 1. Firmus Grid withdraws its landmark AI-infrastructure IPO after investor demand collapses
**Fact**: Nvidia-backed AI data-centre developer Firmus Grid opened an institutional bookbuild on 6 October at A$11/share (implying ~A$43.7bn equity value; some outlets, including CNBC, cite a lower ~A$30.6bn figure using a different basis). Demand from offshore institutional investors was reported weak from the outset. By 7–8 October, underwriters were reportedly exploring cutting the price to around A$8.25–9/share (a ~20–25% reduction) to clear the book. On 8–9 October, Firmus withdrew its ASX listing application entirely, citing "market volatility" and "prevailing market conditions," and said the offer terms would not reflect the strength of its business; it will now pursue capital from private markets and "consider alternative public and private market options." Reported reasons for the investor pullback include: the gap between Firmus's marketed 912MW data-centre development pipeline and its actual built-and-operating capacity (reported at ~46MW); FY26 revenue reported at only ~A$51m against the implied valuation; and escrow/overhang terms that would have let existing holders sell more than half their stock immediately post-listing. Firmus had raised roughly US$2bn in an August private round (Nvidia, Coatue Management, Blackstone, Jane Street and others), at a valuation variously reported as US$10.5bn–US$15bn — meaning the attempted IPO price implied close to a tripling of that valuation within roughly two months.
**Analysis**: this is the most consequential Australian financial-market event of the week by a wide margin. It directly tests — and so far fails — the thesis, advanced by the Macquarie/GIC/Anthropic "Theseus Infrastructure" platform (covered in this project's first report, August 2026) and reinforced by Firmus's own initial roadshow enthusiasm, that Australian/Asia-Pacific capital markets can absorb AI-infrastructure risk at scale. It also directly validates, within one week, the RBA's Financial Stability Review warning (1 October, covered last week) that AI-related financing is "becoming more opaque and more vulnerable to profits falling short of expectations" — this is no longer an abstract central-bank concern but a concrete, public failed transaction.
**Inference**: the mismatch between marketed pipeline (912MW) and built capacity (~46MW) suggests the IPO was priced on a development story rather than demonstrated operating economics; whether this reflects a company-specific disclosure/execution gap or a broader repricing of AI-infrastructure risk across the sector is not yet resolved by available reporting, and this report does not take a view on which explanation dominates.
**Watch next**: whether Firmus successfully raises a new private round (and at what valuation relative to the withdrawn IPO price); whether Maas Group's other Firmus-linked disclosures surface further mark-downs; whether the Firmus outcome chills other AI-infrastructure capital-raising plans in Australia.

### 2. RBA minutes reveal a hawkier rationale than headline coverage suggested: the neutral rate itself may have risen
**Fact**: The RBA published the minutes of its 28–29 September Monetary Policy Board meeting on 13 October (the scheduled two-week-after-decision release). The minutes state that the Board judged a possible rise in the global neutral interest rate meant the prior 4.35% cash rate setting was less restrictive than it had been roughly two years earlier — i.e., the Board's own policy stance may have been doing less work than assumed even before this hike. Three upside inflation risks flagged in August were assessed as having "materialised or were building": broader Middle East conflict effects on energy costs, cost pressures linked to the global AI investment boom, and tighter-than-assumed domestic capacity. Staff also raised the possibility that the unemployment rate consistent with target inflation (the NAIRU) is higher than previously thought, implying more excess demand in the economy than the Board had been assuming. The Board's written conclusion reiterated it will do what is necessary to return inflation to target sustainably, "including increasing the cash rate target further if required." As of the minutes' release, market pricing implied roughly a one-in-three probability of a hike at the 3–4 November meeting and just over 50% for December.
**Analysis**: the "AI boom" being cited by the RBA itself as a source of domestic inflationary cost pressure — not just a financial-stability risk (as in last week's FSR) — is a notable escalation in how directly the central bank is now linking AI-driven investment/compute demand to the inflation outlook. Combined with the neutral-rate reassessment, this is a materially more hawkish signal than the simple "fourth hike of the year" headline conveyed at the time of the 29 September decision.
**Inference**: if the Board genuinely believes the neutral rate has risen, a 4.60% cash rate may need to go higher simply to maintain the same degree of restrictiveness — independent of any fresh inflation surprise. This is this report's reading of the minutes' logic, not a stated RBA forecast, and the Board itself gave only a conditional ("if required") commitment.
**Watch next**: the RBA's 3–4 November decision and accompanching Statement on Monetary Policy; the September-quarter CPI print (due 28 October) as the key swing input.

### 4. Metrics Credit Partners launches independent governance review; KPMG sign-off remains outstanding

Metrics remains the far larger and more consequential case, and its own resolution timeline (KPMG sign-off, independent governance review findings) is still pending.

### 5. Strait of Hormuz: the ceasefire has broken down, not merely remained fragile
**Fact**: Reuters-sourced reporting counted 12 tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz in the week to 5 October 2026 — described as the highest weekly total since the conflict began — including an IRGC Navy strike on the LPG tanker NV Sunshine (9 October) and a projectile strike on a vessel roughly 13 nautical miles west of the UAE that caused a fire. Transit traffic through the Strait remained roughly 75% below pre-conflict levels as of 9 October, per Iran International's reporting. Iran's parliament speaker (Ghalibaf) issued a formal list of preconditions the US must meet before Iran will reopen the Strait; Iran's President separately called ongoing US-mediated talks "meaningless," and Iran stated (10 October) that the latest US response on reopening Hormuz was "insufficient." The US and CENTCOM continue to dispute Iranian claims of control over the waterway, with CENTCOM asserting it has helped move substantial oil volumes out of the Gulf.
**Analysis**: this is an escalation, not the stabilisation some market participants had hoped for after a since-collapsed ceasefire. The 75%-below-baseline transit figure differs materially from the "near-total closure" (1 transit vs. 85/day baseline) figure cited in last week's report from a different data source (IMF PortWatch) — this report flags that discrepancy rather than resolving it, since the two figures may reflect different measurement windows or methodologies.
**Uncertainty**: this report does not take a view on which of the competing transit-volume figures (near-total closure vs. ~75% reduction) is more reliable; both appear in credible reporting from the same general period.
**Watch next**: any further escalation or genuine diplomatic breakthrough ahead of the RBA's 3–4 November decision, given the RBA's own minutes (see #2) cite Middle East energy costs as a live inflation risk.
