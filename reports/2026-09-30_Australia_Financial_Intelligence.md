**Week of 23–30 September 2026**

## Executive Summary

1. **RBA hiked the cash rate 25bp to 4.60% on 29 September** — the fourth increase of 2026 (100bp added year-to-date) and the highest rate in 15 years — reversing the "on hold" narrative from mid-August as inflation outcomes ran hotter than expected, the IMF publicly urged the Board to "stand ready" for more tightening, and — in the Board's own words — a broadening Middle East conflict and AI-driven global demand for technology goods both fed directly into the decision. The big four banks are now split on November: ANZ expects another 25bp hike to 4.85%, CBA calls a November move "live," NAB thinks the RBA is done for now.
2. **Northern Star rejected Gold Fields' ~A$38.7bn ($27.2bn) takeover proposal** on 24 September, calling the heavily scrip-based offer "opportunistic" — the largest M&A story of the week and a live situation, with Gold Fields reportedly weighing a sweetened cash component.
3. **Australia's private-credit sector remains under regulatory and market stress**: following the ~A$3.6bn collapse of Sydney developer Bathla Group in late August, ASIC escalated from warnings to action on 22 September with an interim stop order halting three Remara private-credit products (via Melbourne Securities Corporation) over deficient target-market determinations — a live test of a fast-growing, lightly supervised asset class.
4. **The ASX 200 had a weak week, closing near its lowest level since June** (8,665 as of 25 Sept), before a sharp rally into the RBA decision on 29 September; tech and real estate were the worst-performing sectors over the month amid rising bond yields (10-year ~5.4%, a 15-year high) and a still-unresolved, and separately escalating, Strait of Hormuz conflict — an active naval blockade of Iranian exports continued even as Saudi flows partly offset the shortfall late in the week. Separately, Nvidia-backed AI data-centre operator Firmus Grid began pre-marketing one of the largest ASX IPOs on record (targeting A$5–7bn, ASX debut planned late October).
5. **Korea–Australia ties advanced on multiple fronts**: the "Team Korea" consortium (KIND, Hyundai E&C, Korea Investment Real Asset Management) signed its first Australian project — a A$380m Build-to-Rent tower in Chatswood, Sydney (22 Sept); President Lee Jae Myung and PM Albanese agreed on 23 September (UNGA sidelines) to deepen energy-supply-chain cooperation; Korea Zinc's Richmond Valley renewable-energy investment had its payment date fall on 30 September; and — a genuinely live, competitive deal — Hanwha Defense USA's ~US$1.2bn bid for ASX-listed Austal Limited's US shipbuilding subsidiary now faces a higher rival offer, with the Australian government confirming on 16 September it would not stand in the way of the Korean bidder. Korean capital is now visibly diversifying from resources into real estate, energy-transition infrastructure and industrial M&A.

## Top Developments

### 1. RBA hikes to 4.60% — the hawkish pivot the August report flagged as a risk, now explicitly linked to AI-driven demand
**Fact**: On 29 September, the RBA's Monetary Policy Board unanimously raised the cash rate by 25bp to 4.60%, the fourth hike of 2026 and the highest level since 2011. The Board's statement said inflation remained elevated and upside risks flagged in August were materialising: the Middle East conflict had broadened and global energy prices were running well above the RBA's August forecasts, while — stated explicitly for the first time — rapid AI-related investment among Australia's trading partners was pushing up global prices for technology-related goods, adding to existing domestic capacity pressure. Governor Bullock said the Board considered both a pause and a 25bp move, and warned a "difficult" period with higher unemployment is possible if the AI-driven boom keeps demanding this kind of policy response. This followed an August hold and three earlier 2026 hikes, and came after an IMF Article IV mission (16–17 September) explicitly told the RBA to "stand ready" for more tightening given upside inflation risk.
**Analysis**: this is a materially different picture from the RBA's 11 August posture (hold, cautious-but-patient) reported in the prior weekly report. Two shocks compounded rather than faded as previously hoped: the Strait of Hormuz standoff turned into an active shooting war that has cut Iranian exports and driven Brent above $100/barrel at times, and — new this cycle — the RBA is now treating AI capex/demand as a first-order, persistent inflation channel rather than a one-off. This is one of the more explicit statements globally by a G20-adjacent central bank linking the AI investment boom directly to monetary tightening, and the IMF's public "stand ready" language likely reinforced the Board's own reading of incoming data.
**Inference**: markets are pricing roughly a 40–45% chance of a further hike in November; if the AI-demand channel persists (semiconductor/hardware price pressure, data-centre-driven energy demand), the RBA's reaction function may stay tighter for longer than markets had priced, independent of how the Iran conflict resolves. This is our inference, not a stated RBA reaction function.
**Watch next**: whether the November Statement on Monetary Policy quantifies the AI-inflation channel, and whether the big banks' economics teams (ANZ hawkish at 4.85% by year-end; CBA calls a November move "live"; NAB thinks the RBA is done) converge or diverge further — Q3 CPI (due late October) will be the key swing input.

### 2. Mortgage pass-through begins; bank sector split on the path ahead
**Fact**: Ahead of the RBA decision, ANZ, NAB, Westpac and Macquarie Bank all lifted fixed mortgage rates in September (moves of 15–50bp across various terms/LVRs), front-running rather than waiting for the RBA. Macquarie Bank confirmed it will pass the full 25bp increase on to variable home-loan rates from 15 October; other major lenders are expected to follow within days. The cumulative effect of 2026's four hikes adds roughly A$364/month to repayments on a A$600,000 loan.
**Analysis**: this directly extends the margin-pressure theme flagged in the prior report (Westpac's ~20% mortgage-application drop in August). Lenders moving ahead of, rather than after, the RBA signals the market now treats further tightening as the base case, not a tail risk — a shift from the "will the RBA even hike again" framing of August to "how many more hikes." A fifth month of national house-price declines combined with a fourth rate hike is a genuinely new stress test for household demand and bank credit growth, not yet a confirmed downturn.
**What to watch**: October housing-finance data and the big four's commentary for signs of slowing new lending or rising arrears.

### 3. Northern Star rejects Gold Fields' ~A$38.7bn takeover proposal
**Fact**: Gold Fields submitted a non-binding, indicative proposal on 13–14 September to acquire 100% of Northern Star Resources via scheme of arrangement, implying ~A$27/share (a 22% premium to the pre-approach close), of which roughly 73% was Gold Fields scrip and 27% cash — Northern Star shareholders would have held ~33% of the combined group. Northern Star's board formally rejected the approach on 24–27 September (reported both dates across sources), calling it "opportunistic" given the scrip-heavy structure, sending its shares up as much as 9%. Gold Fields is reportedly now weighing a sweetened, more cash-heavy bid.
**Analysis**: this would create the world's second-largest gold miner and comes as gold trades near record highs (~US$4,150–4,400/oz) on Fed-cut expectations, Hormuz-driven risk-off flows and record central-bank buying — Northern Star's rejection suggests the board believes its standalone re-rating potential in a structurally higher gold-price environment exceeds what a scrip-heavy bid captures, not that the strategic logic is flawed.
**What to watch**: whether Gold Fields returns with a materially higher cash component, whether the deal (if it proceeds) draws FIRB scrutiny given the scale and a foreign acquirer, and whether this catalyses further consolidation among ASX-listed gold miners (Evolution, Regis, Genesis, Capricorn all rallied alongside the sector this month).

### 4. ASIC escalates from warnings to action: interim stop order halts three Remara private-credit products
**Fact**: On 22 September, ASIC issued a 21-day interim stop order (26-225MR) against three private-credit products offered under the Remara Cash Management Fund by Melbourne Securities Corporation (the 6-month, 12-month and At Call accounts), citing deficient target market determinations — the products had been marketed to retail investors seeking "capital preservation" and a "low risk" rating despite allocating up to 75% to a single fund as a "major component." This followed ASIC's 21 September public warning (in the context of the ~A$3.4–3.6bn Bathla Group collapse) that the private-credit sector should expect more enforcement, and sits alongside continuing fallout from Bathla itself — roughly 40 private-credit funds remain exposed (PAG ~A$340m, CVS Lane ~A$250m among the largest), with several (Centuria Bass, CVS Lane) having frozen investor redemptions. APRA said bank and pension exposure to Bathla specifically was limited.
**Analysis**: this is a step change from the priority-setting and warnings described in the prior report — ASIC has now taken concrete enforcement action (not just rhetoric) against a private-credit product for mis-describing its risk profile to retail investors, a more consequential regulatory signal than a general warning.
**Inference**: expect ASIC to use the Remara stop order as a template for further target-market-determination reviews across other retail-facing private-credit funds; this is a plausible near-term regulatory trajectory based on ASIC's own stated framing, not yet an announced sector-wide policy.

### 6. Hanwha's US$1.2bn bid for Austal USA becomes a competitive process; Canberra confirms it won't block a Korean buyer
**Fact**: Hanwha Defense USA (part of South Korea's Hanwha Group) submitted a non-binding proposal in August to acquire the US business/shipyard operations of ASX-listed Austal Limited (Mobile, Alabama) for an enterprise value of US$1.05–1.20bn, explicitly excluding Austal's Australian, Philippine and Vietnamese operations and requiring US CFIUS/DCSA approval. **Why it matters for Korean financial institutions/investors**: the outcome (Hanwha vs. Wildcat, and CFIUS's eventual view) will be a useful precedent for how comfortably Korean industrial and financial capital can compete for US-exposed assets that happen to sit inside Australian-listed corporate structures.

## Regulation & Policy

- **RBA** — 25bp hike to 4.60%, first explicit statement linking AI-driven global demand for tech goods to Australian inflation risk (see #1).
- **ASIC's digital-asset licensing deadline landed on 30 September 2026** — the last day of its extended no-action position for crypto/digital-asset businesses. Firms that have not applied for or varied an AFS licence (or an Australian Market Licence/CS facility licence where relevant) by this date operate in breach of the law from 1 October, with penalties of up to 10% of annual turnover — a hard compliance deadline directly relevant to any institution (Korean or otherwise) operating digital-asset services into Australia.
- **ASIC private-credit enforcement** (21–22 September) — see Top Developments #4; this sits alongside ASIC's previously stated FY26 priority areas (misleading pricing, private credit, insurance complaints, financial reporting).
- **APRA/ASIC — Financial Accountability Regime (FAR) streamlining**: a consultation letter released 2 September proposes raising materiality thresholds for notifying accountability changes and removing some FAR reporting requirements for ~4,500 accountable persons/entities, with implementation targeted by end-2026.
- **APRA/ASIC — frontier AI risk**: both regulators have, over 2026, escalated warnings (APRA's 30 April letter to industry; ASIC's 8 May cyber-resilience call; joint 1 September superannuation CEO roundtable notes) that AI/cyber governance at the largest banks, insurers and super trustees is "failing to keep pace" with adoption — a persistent, escalating supervisory theme that now intersects directly with the RBA's new AI-inflation framing (regulators and the central bank both treating the AI boom as a first-order risk vector, from different angles).
- **IMF Article IV mission (16–17 September)** — recommended the RBA "stand ready" for further hikes and urged the government to rein in spending to support disinflation, projecting growth slowing to 1.9% in 2026 and 1.6% in 2027. IMF staff advice, not government or RBA policy, but a material input into how markets read the subsequent 29 September hike.

## Emerging Trends

- **AI investment as a two-sided risk for Australian finance**: simultaneously an inflation channel the RBA now names explicitly (#1), a governance/cyber risk APRA/ASIC are escalating warnings on, and a capital-markets opportunity (Theseus, Firmus Grid's IPO). This is the clearest cross-cutting theme of the quarter and merits continued tracking across all three lenses.
- **The rate cycle has re-accelerated, not paused.** The prior report's base case (RBA on hold) proved directionally right on timing but wrong on outcome — the Board hiked rather than held, and lenders front-ran the decision. Bank forecasters are now genuinely split on November, an unusual degree of public disagreement among the big four's own economics teams.
- **Private credit's first real stress test is underway.** Bathla is the largest of several recent private-debt-linked corporate failures and has moved ASIC from priority-setting to explicit enforcement within weeks — the beginning of a tighter oversight cycle for a segment that grew rapidly while banks retreated from higher-risk property lending.
- **Gold and gold-miner consolidation.** Record gold prices are now colliding with major-miner M&A (Northern Star/Gold Fields) — likely to continue if the metal holds near record levels into Q4.
- **Physical-commodity trading re-consolidation.** Macquarie's active pursuit of metals-trading books suggests global commodity-trading capacity is consolidating toward large financial-institution balance sheets amid continued volatility (Iran war, gold M&A).
- **Korean capital's shift from resources toward real estate, energy-transition infrastructure and industrial M&A.** The Team Korea Chatswood BTR deal, Korea Zinc's Richmond Valley investment, POSCO's lithium stake and now Hanwha's competitive bid for Austal USA together suggest Korean strategic and industrial capital is broadening well beyond critical-minerals extraction — consistent with, and now backed by, the leader-level 23 September energy statement.
