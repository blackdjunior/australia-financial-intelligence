### 1. RBA's 3 November minutes (published 17 Nov) reveal persistently hawkish reasoning behind the 6-3 split hike
**Fact**: the Reserve Bank published the minutes of its 3 November Monetary Policy Board meeting on 17 November 2026. The minutes confirm the previously reported 6-3 split vote to raise the cash rate 25bp to 4.85%. The majority's reasoning, per secondary synthesis of the minutes (rba.gov.au itself was not directly accessible in this research session): the Board judged that the larger risk was leaving policy "insufficiently restrictive," noting inflation had been above target "for an extended period" and could prove "more persistent than anticipated." Staff forecasts were revised upward — headline inflation is now seen remaining above 3.0% until late 2027, with a return to the 2.5% target midpoint not expected until mid-2028. The majority also flagged that the neutral policy rate "may have shifted higher," implying the current 4.85% setting delivers less restraint than its headline level suggests. The RBA's own forecasts reportedly assume Brent crude falls roughly US$30/bbl over the next two years — a disinflationary assumption this report flags as a direct risk given the unresolved Strait of Hormuz situation (see #6 below). The three dissenting members reportedly cited the unemployment rate, which stood at 4.7% in the September ABS Labour Force release (a pre-existing figure, not new this week), and the risk of a sharper-than-expected downturn. Market reaction, per secondary reporting, was to price out a December hike almost entirely; one economist cited expects the Board's next move to be a cut, but not until Q4 2027.
**Analysis**: this materially sharpens, rather than merely confirms, last week's framing of the 6-3 vote. A narrow majority alone could have been read as a one-off, finely balanced call; the minutes instead show the majority holding a structurally more hawkish inflation view (a full year-plus longer above-target path than the dissenters implicitly accept) and introducing a new, more durable hawkish argument — a higher neutral rate — that does not depend on any single data print. This raises the bar for any near-term easing and is consistent with, not contradicted by, this week's separately released consumer-sentiment weakness (#7 below): the Board appears willing to tolerate household-level pain to anchor inflation expectations.
**Uncertainty**: this report could not directly access rba.gov.au in this research session and relies on secondary paraphrase of the minutes' exact language; the precise wording of the "neutral rate" and "insufficiently restrictive" framing should be treated as reported characterisation rather than verified direct quotation until confirmed against the primary document.
**Watch next**: any RBA speech or commentary between now and the 8 December decision that either confirms or walks back the "neutral rate has shifted" framing; whether the October Labour Force release (due 19 November, just outside this window) changes the calculus for the dissenting minority's case.

### 2. Bathla Group's A$3.4bn collapse becomes the live test case for ASIC's private-credit enforcement campaign
**Fact**: Bathla Group, a Western Sydney homebuilder, was placed into voluntary administration (Teneo appointed) in early November, with reported debts of approximately A$3.2-3.4bn owed across more than 42 lenders. ASIC Commissioner Simone Constant announced on 10 November a formal investigation into Bathla entities for possible wrongdoing contributing to the collapse, working with administrators to trace the loaned funds and assess retail-investor exposure; ASIC Chair Sarah Court described the matter as reflecting "troubling developments" in private credit, and Constant called it a "concerning and dark case study." On 9 November, ASIC issued interim stop orders (26-271MR) against two Angas Securities-managed mortgage funds — the Angas Prime Income Fund and Angas Direct Mortgage Fund — alleging failure to adequately disclose Bathla-related exposure and risk in the funds' product disclosure statements. Angas's executive chairman publicly disputed the basis for the order, attributing the apparent exposure figure to a data-transcription error and stating the firm expects resolution by end-December while mid-way through a capital raise. Separately, ASIC released a private-credit sector surveillance report (26-273MR, based on Report 846) finding that only 2 of 22 assessed private-credit funds passed the full economic benefit of interest and borrower fees through to investors, and identifying at least one case of a financial adviser recommending an 87% single-fund portfolio concentration — the highest observed. The release explicitly ties Fidante Partners, Australian Secure Capital Fund, Melbourne Securities Corporation (3 orders, via the Remara Cash Management Fund) and Murdoch Clarke Mortgage Management's prior stop orders to this same surveillance programme. ASIC's Annual Forum was held 17-18 November at ICC Sydney, with its 2027 enforcement priorities (expected to include private credit again) on the agenda; the outcome of that announcement had not been reported as of this research session.
**Analysis**: this is the first concrete real-world loss event this report series has found connected to ASIC's year-long pattern of private-credit TMD stop orders, which until now had been a purely regulatory/disclosure story without a confirmed underlying blow-up. Bathla's scale (A$3.4bn, 42+ lenders) and the 2-of-22 fee-pass-through finding together suggest the private-credit enforcement campaign is not regulatory overreach into a healthy sector but a response to genuinely weak investor-protection practices at scale.
**Inference**: the Angas dispute over the stop order's factual basis (a claimed data error versus ASIC's reading) is a useful reminder that not every interim stop order reflects a confirmed, serious breach — some will likely be resolved in the regulated entity's favour — but the broader surveillance finding (fee pass-through, concentration risk) is not contingent on that specific dispute's outcome.
**Uncertainty**: this report could not directly access asic.gov.au in this session and relies on wire-service syndication (AAP/Reuters via multiple regional papers) and secondary trade-press coverage for exact dates and figures; the precise date of 26-273MR's release could not be confirmed as falling inside versus just before the 12-18 November window, and the scale of Bathla's investor-facing (as opposed to lender-facing) losses is not yet established.
**Watch next**: ASIC's 2027 enforcement priorities announcement from the 17-18 November Annual Forum; resolution of the Angas interim stop order (standard 21-day term, due to lapse around 30 November); any further Bathla-linked fund disclosures.

### 3. PwC's Major Banks Analysis: big-four earnings grow fastest since FY23, but non-majors are winning mortgage share
**Fact**: PwC released its "Major Banks Analysis: Full Year 2026" on 12 November, finding the big four's combined cash earnings rose 8.1% to A$32.5bn in FY26 — the strongest growth rate since FY23 — with total lending up 5.4% to A$3.7tn. The report highlighted that non-major banks and mutuals grew housing credit 8.3% and 9.8% respectively to September 2026, versus 4.5% for the majors, pointing to accelerating market-share loss in home lending. PwC's analysis also highlighted AI and fraud-prevention adoption as a differentiator, and its chief economist flagged continued Middle East fuel-supply disruption as a live risk to future provisioning. This followed KPMG's own "Big Four Major Banks Australia Full Year Results 2026" report, released 9 November, which put combined major-bank profit after tax at A$31.2bn (+4.7% on FY25) and return on equity up 40bp to 11.1% — a different headline figure from PwC's A$32.5bn, which this report flags as a methodology/scope/timing discrepancy rather than attempting to reconcile.
**Analysis**: taken together with last week's NAB/ANZ results and the Westpac result from two weeks prior, this is the clearest primary-adjacent synthesis yet that the "impairments rising sector-wide" story and the "majors still growing profit solidly" story are not contradictory — credit losses are normalising off an unusually low base even as aggregate earnings grow — but the market-share data adds a third, previously under-emphasised thread: competitive intensity in mortgages is now visibly reallocating volume away from the majors toward smaller lenders, which is a different and potentially more structural pressure on major-bank net interest income than cyclical impairment normalisation.
**Uncertainty**: the KPMG/PwC figure discrepancy ($31.2bn vs $32.5bn for ostensibly the same FY26 cohort) is unresolved; this report does not assert which, if either, figure is more authoritative.
**Watch next**: whether major-bank management commentary at upcoming investor days addresses the non-major mortgage market-share loss directly; any further analyst reconciliation of the KPMG/PwC figures.

## Emerging Trends

- **Private-credit enforcement has moved from a disclosure-compliance story to a real-loss story.** ASIC's pattern of interim stop orders over the past several reports had, until this week, lacked a confirmed underlying investor-loss event. Bathla Group's A$3.4bn collapse and the accompanying surveillance finding (2 of 22 funds passing fee pass-through tests; an 87% single-fund concentration recommendation) together suggest the enforcement campaign responds to a genuinely weak sector, not regulatory overreach — a materially stronger basis for ASIC's stated 2026-27 private-credit priority than this report series had previously been able to establish.
- **Major-bank earnings growth and major-bank market share are now diverging.** PwC's finding that non-major banks and mutuals are growing housing credit nearly twice as fast as the majors, even as the majors post their strongest combined earnings growth since FY23, suggests two separate stories are running concurrently: cyclical credit-impairment normalisation (tracked in prior reports) and a more structural competitive erosion in mortgages. These should not be conflated into a single "bank margin pressure" narrative.
- **The oil-price/Hormuz-attack-tempo divergence has now flipped direction in successive weeks**, a genuine instability in how markets are pricing an unresolved geopolitical risk rather than a one-off anomaly. This is also a direct, if modest, risk to the RBA's own disinflationary oil-price assumption underpinning its 3 November forecasts.
- **AI-infrastructure leadership risk has entered the picture for the first time.** Since August, this report has tracked institutional capital (Macquarie/GIC/Anthropic's Theseus Infrastructure) moving into AI data-centre assets as a structural, not speculative, theme. Nvidia's sudden leadership change is the first event in this series directly introducing governance/leadership uncertainty at the top of that supply chain, with a visible (if imprecisely measured) ASX read-through to data-centre-linked names.
