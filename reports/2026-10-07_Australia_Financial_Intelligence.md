## Executive Summary

1. **Metrics Credit Partners — one of Australia's largest ASX-listed private-credit managers (~A$30bn AUM) — froze more than A$9bn across five funds** after auditor KPMG refused to sign off on FY26 accounts over disputed property/loan valuations, cutting net asset backing by up to ~12% in one fund. Bloomberg reported ASIC has opened an investigation into Metrics' loan valuations and governance; Metrics says it is not aware of any such probe. This is a materially larger and more systemically important event than the Remara stop order flagged in last week's report — and it broke just days after the RBA's own Financial Stability Review called non-bank lender risk "contained by their relatively small size."

3. **Northern Star / Gold Fields remains unresolved.** Northern Star shares jumped ~8% on 30 September on reports Gold Fields is weighing a sweetened, more cash-heavy bid, but no formal revised offer had been lodged as of 7 October; investors expect a move only after Gold Fields' Australian roadshow in late October.
4. **Firmus Grid priced its ASX IPO at A$11/share**, targeting roughly A$7bn raised at an implied ~A$43.7bn valuation — one of the largest listings in Australian history. The institutional bookbuild opened 6 October; retail offer runs 12–19 October; ASX debut is set for 22 October.
5. **A genuinely quiet week for new Korea–Australia developments.** The only concrete, dated item is Korean-language-sourced: Korea Zinc's Ark Energy has pushed back construction and a A$400bn-equivalent capital call on its Richmond Valley (NSW) solar-and-battery project by roughly two months. The Hanwha-vs-Wildcat Infrastructure bidding contest for Austal USA remains open and unresolved, with no binding deal reported.

## Top Developments

### 1. Metrics Credit Partners freezes over A$9bn after KPMG audit dispute; Bloomberg reports an ASIC investigation
**Fact**: On 28 September, Metrics Credit Partners — one of Australia's largest private-credit managers, with roughly A$30bn under management — placed three ASX-listed funds (Metrics Master Income Trust/MXT, Metrics Income Opportunities Trust/MOT, Metrics Real Estate Multi-Strategy Fund/MRE) into a trading halt after auditor KPMG made different valuation and scenario-likelihood judgements than Metrics had used in its preliminary FY26 accounts, particularly on unlisted commercial-real-estate equity. Net tangible asset backing was cut by roughly 12% at MRE, ~10% at MOT and ~2% at MXT (one outlet put the aggregate writedown near A$170m). The halt and a related suspension of the listed funds from quotation continued through 30 September; Metrics separately suspended distribution reinvestment plans for MXT and MOT from 30 September (distributions now paid in cash). On the same date, Metrics also froze withdrawals on two large unlisted wholesale vehicles — the ~A$6bn Metrics Wholesale Investment Trust and the ~A$5.4bn MCP Real Estate Debt Fund — after KPMG said it would not sign off on their accounts by the 30 September statutory deadline either, for a 90-day period. Separately, Bloomberg reported that ASIC has opened an investigation into Metrics specifically over loan valuations and governance practices as part of a broader private-credit sector probe; Metrics issued a media statement saying it is "not aware of any ASIC investigation related to specific practices or transactions" in its business, while ASIC declined to comment on Metrics by name but confirmed it is increasing surveillance of private-equity and private-credit fund valuation and governance practices.
**Analysis**: this is a step change from the Remara/Bathla stress covered in the 30 September report. Remara was a retail mis-selling action against three small products; Metrics is a flagship, ASX-listed manager with a public market capitalisation, freezing over A$9bn across five vehicles on its own auditor's objection to its valuations — a governance and credibility event for the sector's largest players, not just its fringes. It lands only three weeks after ASIC Commissioner Simone Constant told the private-credit industry (22 September, CAFBA summit) to "prepare for enforcement action" because the regulator is "well beyond warnings," and specifically criticised super-fund CIOs for not vetting managers against ASIC's ten principles of private credit.
**Inference**: a listed fund freezing on an auditor's valuation objection — rather than on a borrower default — suggests the immediate issue is governance/disclosure rather than (yet) a credit-loss event; whether it proves to be the former or evolves into the latter is unresolved and this report takes no view. This is our inference, not a confirmed fact.
**Watch next**: whether KPMG ultimately signs the FY26 accounts and on what terms; whether ASIC confirms a formal Metrics investigation; whether other large private-credit managers face comparable audit disputes (a sector-wide, not single-manager, question); and whether superannuation funds with Metrics exposure disclose write-downs.

### 2. RBA's October Financial Stability Review calls the system resilient — published days before Metrics unravelled
**Fact**: On 1 October, the RBA released its half-yearly Financial Stability Review. Primary conclusions: the Australian financial system "has a good degree of resilience" and "no room for complacency"; most mortgaged households have positive equity and savings buffers; banks are "well capitalised" and able to keep lending through a downturn; and non-bank lender (private-credit) risk is described as "contained by their relatively small size" relative to the broader system. The RBA separately flagged that risks are "increasingly coming from abroad" — elevated sovereign debt, high asset prices, leverage — and specifically warned that "the boom in long-term debt financing to support AI investments is becoming more opaque and more vulnerable to profits falling short of expectations," alongside a more challenging cyber-threat landscape.
**Analysis**: the FSR's sanguine framing of private-credit risk sits uncomfortably next to the Metrics freeze that broke the same week (#1) — not a contradiction (Metrics is a governance/valuation dispute, not yet a demonstrated credit loss, and the private-credit sector remains small relative to bank balance sheets), but a reminder that "contained by size" is a system-level, not fund-level, claim, and it does not rule out individual manager-level shocks with reputational spillover. The RBA's own AI-financing-opacity warning is directly relevant to the Firmus Grid IPO pricing the same week (#4).
**Watch next**: whether the RBA's November Statement on Monetary Policy or minutes revisit non-bank lending risk in light of Metrics; whether APRA follows with its own commentary on bank exposure to private-credit funds.

### 3. Strait of Hormuz: shipping effectively halted, not merely disrupted
**Fact**: IMF PortWatch data cited this week show only one transit through the Strait of Hormuz on 27 September, versus an 85-per-day pre-crisis baseline. At least six vessels were reported struck in the Strait in the week to ~3 October; the Kuwait Oil Tanker Co.'s Kazimah III was hit by a projectile roughly five miles off Oman on 1 October, suffering a small fire and blackout before continuing under its own power. US officials have continued to describe the US naval blockade response as able to be sustained indefinitely. Brent crude was trading around US$92/bbl on 6 October — down from the $100+ levels reported two weeks earlier.
**Analysis**: "near-total closure of the Strait to commercial shipping" is a materially different, more severe fact than "ongoing disruption," the framing used in the prior two reports. It is consistent with a structural shift toward non-Hormuz routing (the Saudi pipeline workaround flagged previously) rather than genuine de-escalation.
**Uncertainty**: the combination of near-zero Hormuz throughput and a Brent price below its late-September peak is not fully reconciled by available reporting — plausible explanations include sustained non-Hormuz supply (Saudi pipeline, strategic reserves) or demand-side softening, but this report does not take a view on which dominates and flags the apparent tension rather than resolving it.

### 4. Northern Star / Gold Fields: still no revised offer, but the market is pricing one in
**Fact**: Northern Star's board unanimously rejected Gold Fields' unsolicited ~A$38.7bn (0.3125 new Gold Fields shares + A$7.25 cash per share) proposal in late September. On 30 September, Northern Star shares rose roughly 8% to A$25.18 on Bloomberg reports that Gold Fields is weighing sweetening the cash component of its proposal, with investors briefed by Gold Fields expecting an improved offer — "most likely with more cash" — following an Australian investor roadshow expected in late October. As of 7 October, a source familiar with the process said no decision had been made on raising the offer and there is no guarantee a revised proposal will arrive.
**Analysis**: this remains the largest live M&A situation on the ASX, but the gap between market expectation (an improved bid is coming) and confirmed fact (nothing has been lodged) is wide enough that this report treats it as unresolved rather than imminent.
**Watch next**: the outcome of Gold Fields' late-October roadshow and whether a formal revised, cash-heavier proposal follows.

### 5. Firmus Grid prices its ASX IPO at A$11/share, targeting one of Australia's largest-ever listings
**Fact**: Nvidia-backed, Blackstone-backed AI data-centre developer Firmus Grid (customers reportedly include Nvidia, Meta and OpenAI) opened its institutional bookbuild on 6 October at an indicative price of A$11/share, targeting a raise of roughly A$7bn (with reports of a possible upsize via a ~A$500m over-allotment option) at an implied valuation of approximately A$43.7bn. Bankers reportedly had the institutional book already covered at that price. The retail offer is scheduled for 12–19 October, with an ASX trading debut targeted for 22 October — brought forward from the "late October" guidance in last week's report. Separately, Firmus's draft prospectus reportedly forecasts a A$77m first-half loss.
**Analysis**: this is the second major Australian AI-infrastructure capital-formation event in two months (after Macquarie/GIC/Anthropic's Theseus Infrastructure platform) and, if it prices and lists as planned, would rank among the largest Australian IPOs ever — a direct test of public-market, not just private-institutional, appetite for AI-infrastructure risk, arriving in the same week the RBA's own FSR flagged AI-financing opacity as a financial-stability risk (#2).
**Watch next**: final pricing, retail-offer demand, and the 22 October debut performance.

## Emerging Trends

- **Private credit's stress test has moved from the sector's fringes to its largest listed players.** Bathla (a borrower collapse) and Remara (a mis-sold retail product) were weaknesses at the edges of the private-credit market; Metrics Credit Partners is a ~A$30bn, ASX-listed flagship manager freezing funds on its own auditor's objection. Whether this is contained to one manager's governance practices or signals a broader valuation-discipline problem across the sector is the single most important open question for Australian credit markets into Q4.
- **A visible gap between the RBA's system-level reassurance and fund-level reality.** The RBA's Financial Stability Review called non-bank lender risk "contained by size" days before the largest private-credit freeze of the year — not necessarily a contradiction, but a reminder that system-level resilience claims do not preclude individual-manager shocks with reputational and confidence effects beyond their formal size.
- **The Hormuz risk regime has changed in kind, not just degree** — from disruption to near-total closure by transit-count — even as spot oil prices have not moved proportionally, an unresolved puzzle worth tracking into the RBA's November decision.
- **AI infrastructure capital formation continues even as the RBA flags its own financing opacity as a risk.** Firmus Grid's successful (if not yet fully priced) institutional bookbuild lands in the same week the RBA's FSR specifically warned that AI-related long-term debt financing is "becoming more opaque and more vulnerable to profits falling short of expectations" — a direct, if implicit, tension between capital-markets enthusiasm and the central bank's own risk assessment.
- **AI adoption inside the banks continues to scale operationally** (Westpac's AI agents in loan workflows, CBA's AI fraud detection) even without fresh announcements this week — this remains a standing, multi-quarter theme rather than a one-off story.
