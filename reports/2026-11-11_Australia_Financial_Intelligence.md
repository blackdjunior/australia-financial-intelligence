## Executive Summary

1. **Big-four bank reporting season concluded with NAB (5 Nov) and ANZ (9 Nov) FY26 results, confirming credit impairments are rising sector-wide while margin direction is bank-specific** — NAB's statutory profit fell ~9% with credit impairment charges up ~86% and shares fell ~5-6% (worst day since February 2025), while ANZ's cash profit rose 31% headline (10% ex-items) even as its own impairment charges climbed materially off a smaller base. This refines, rather than confirms, last week's Westpac-led "margin compression + rising bad debts" narrative: impairment deterioration now looks genuinely sector-wide across Westpac, NAB and ANZ, but NAB's net interest margin actually rose 6bp while Westpac's fell 7bp — margin pressure is not uniform.
2. **Last week's reported Strait of Hormuz "data gap" for November was a research-process miss, not a real-world lull** — this week's direct search found a dense, well-sourced run of tanker attacks and incidents from 5–10 November (UKMTO, gCaptain, Al Jazeera, Seatrade Maritime), with the regional threat level held at SEVERE and at least 8 vessels attacked since 1 November. Notably, Brent crude fell across the week anyway, on reports that the US does not plan an imminent strike on Iran post-midterms and that Gulf crude exports are recovering — a genuine disconnect between ground-truth attack tempo and market risk pricing that this report flags rather than resolves.
3. **NextDC shares surged roughly 43-50% on 5 November after Goodman Group and Digital Realty jointly built a 9.9% stake via an after-market bookbuild at A$9.50/share (a ~45% premium), valuing the stake at ~A$715-730m** — the buyers say they have no current intention to bid for the company but reserved the right to, an opportunistic move that follows NextDC's 20 October FY27 guidance downgrade tied to Jemena-caused power-connection delays at its M3 Melbourne data centre.

## Top Developments

### 1. Big-four reporting season concludes: NAB and ANZ FY26 results show impairments rising sector-wide, margins diverging
**Fact**: NAB's FY26 full-year result (released 5 November) showed statutory net profit of approximately $6.14bn, down roughly 9% year-on-year; cash earnings of $6.14bn were down 13.4% on a headline basis, dragged by $949m of after-tax notable items, with underlying cash earnings excluding notables roughly flat at ~$7.1bn. Net interest margin rose 6bp to 1.80%. The credit impairment charge rose sharply — multiple secondary sources put it at roughly $1.5-1.55bn, an increase of about 86% on the prior year (secondary sources differ on the exact prior-year base, cited variously as $424m-$833m, which this report cannot resolve and flags as a sourcing inconsistency rather than asserting a single figure). The final dividend was 85 cents fully franked, taking the full-year total to 170 cents, flat on FY25. NAB guided system housing-credit growth slowing to roughly 2.5-2.6% in FY27 from ~6.7-7.5% in FY26. NAB shares fell approximately 5-6% intraday on 5 November, reportedly their worst day since February 2025 (sources vary on the exact magnitude). ANZ's FY26 result (released 9 November) showed statutory profit of $7.27bn and cash profit of $7.59bn, up 31% on a headline basis (10% excluding FY25 significant items), with cash return on tangible equity up 109bp to 11.56%. ANZ's combined impairment charge was $582m (individual provisions $360m, up $33m year-on-year; collective provisions $222m, up $108m year-on-year) — a materially smaller absolute base than NAB or Westpac, but a clear increase. CET1 was 11.99%, down 4bp year-on-year. The final dividend was 83 cents, 80% franked (up from 75% at the interim), taking the full-year total to 166 cents, flat on FY25. Operating expenses excluding significant items fell 7% to $11.07bn. ANZ's full-year net interest margin figure could not be found in available reporting (only half-year/quarterly data, ~1.54-1.56%); this report flags that figure as unconfirmed rather than estimating it. Both banks' detailed figures above are sourced to secondary coverage (Motley Fool Australia, Market Index, Morningstar Australia, Capital Brief) rather than independently verified against the primary ASX/IR releases, which could not be directly fetched in this research session; this is flagged accordingly.
**Analysis**: this materially refines last week's reading of Westpac's result (NIM down 7bp to ~1.87%, impairments roughly doubled to $864m) as a possible sector-wide pattern. Impairment deterioration now looks genuinely sector-wide — Westpac, NAB and ANZ all show clear, multi-bank increases in credit-loss charges, and KPMG's half-year (HY26) cross-bank data (ANZ $274m vs $145m; NAB $706m vs $348m; Westpac $443m vs $250m; CBA roughly flat) suggests this trend began building well before this week, consistent with gradually normalising credit quality after an extended low-loss period rather than a sudden shock. Margin direction, however, is not sector-wide: NAB's NIM rose while Westpac's fell, which complicates any simple "rate cycle is squeezing all banks equally" narrative and suggests idiosyncratic competitive and funding-mix factors are at least as important as the cash-rate level itself.
**Inference**: ANZ's strong headline profit growth, delivered despite rising impairments, suggests its "ANZ 2030" cost-out strategy and provisioning base are currently better positioned than NAB's to absorb credit normalisation without a profit hit — though one analyst was quoted describing the result as "really a nothing result," implying the market sees it as more a confirmation of expectations than a positive surprise.
**Uncertainty**: KPMG's "Big Four Major Banks Australia Full Year Results 2026" report, scheduled for release around 9 November, would be the best primary analyst synthesis of this question but could not be retrieved in this research session; NAB's CET1 ratio (~11.87%, single-source) and ANZ's full-year NIM and share-price reaction on 9 November could not be independently confirmed.

### 2. Strait of Hormuz: the "data gap" was a search miss, not a lull — confirmed escalation through 5-10 November, with oil prices moving the opposite way
**Fact**: last week's report found no November-dated Strait of Hormuz incident reporting and flagged that as a notable, if unresolved, finding. This week's direct, repeated search instead turned up a dense, multiply-sourced run of dated incidents: a 5 November UKMTO-reported explosion roughly 16nm east of Khasab, Oman; a 7 November incident 8nm northwest of Khasab in which the tanker Navig8 Messi (ADNOC L&S-linked) caught fire and its crew evacuated, with Iran's navy separately claiming three vessels attempting "unauthorised" transit suffered explosions or fire; US CENTCOM reportedly disabling the bulker Huda over the 7-8 November weekend after repeated attempts to breach a US naval blockade of Iranian ports (Seatrade Maritime put the cumulative tally at 8 vessels attacked since 1 November); continued IRGC targeting of oil tankers reported 8 November, with the Joint Maritime Information Centre (JMIC) holding the Hormuz threat level at SEVERE; and a further 10 November incident in which ADNOC L&S confirmed a vessel damaged in a Persian Gulf attack, alongside a separate UKMTO report of a tanker struck by an unidentified projectile near Lima, Oman. Separately, Brent crude fell across the week — from roughly $103.60/bbl on 3 November to a range variously reported between $93 and $99/bbl by 5-6 November (sources disagree on the exact weekly percentage decline, citing figures between 12% and 18%) — reportedly on comments that the US does not plan an imminent strike on Iran following the midterm elections, and on reports that Gulf crude exports are recovering toward pre-conflict levels. Qatar-mediated US-Iran talks over a reported seven-step Iranian proposal (Hormuz reopening in exchange for an end to the US naval blockade, an oil-waiver, and sanctions relief) continued, but no confirmed breakthrough or formal ceasefire dated within 5-11 November was found; reporting on the talks' status is contradictory.
**Analysis**: the correction itself is a useful process lesson — this report's own prior-week search evidently missed available coverage rather than genuine news being absent, and this week's broader, more persistent search found it. Substantively, the fact that oil prices fell through the week even as physical attacks continued at an elevated, SEVERE-rated tempo is a real and underexplained divergence: markets appear to be pricing a perceived path toward de-escalation (driven by US political signalling and Gulf export-recovery reports) that is not yet reflected in the attack data itself. This matters for the RBA's own inflation calculus — its 3 November statement cited "rising oil prices" as an upside risk, and a falling oil price into November, if sustained, would be a genuine, if modest, disinflationary offset.
**Uncertainty**: this report does not take a view on whether the oil-price decline or the continued attack tempo is the more reliable signal of where the conflict is headed; no confirmed Brent/WTI price for 9-11 November specifically was found, and the exact weekly percentage move is disputed across sources.

## Emerging Trends

- **Credit-quality normalisation is now visibly a multi-bank, not single-bank, story.** With NAB and ANZ joining Westpac in reporting materially higher impairment charges this FY26 season, and KPMG's half-year cross-bank data suggesting the trend was already building by March 2026, this looks like a genuine, broad-based normalisation of credit losses off an unusually low base — rather than an idiosyncratic Westpac issue, as last week's report could not yet determine. Margin direction, however, is not moving in lockstep (NAB's NIM rose, Westpac's fell), which argues against a single simple "higher-for-longer rates squeeze everyone equally" story and toward more bank-specific competitive and funding dynamics.
- **A research-process lesson worth recording explicitly**: last week's report treated an absence of November-dated Strait of Hormuz coverage as a genuinely ambiguous finding (lull vs. reporting lag vs. search miss) and declined to guess which. This week's broader, more persistent search resolved it clearly as a search miss — the conflict continued at an elevated, SEVERE-rated tempo throughout. The actionable lesson is to treat an initial "nothing found" result for an active, ongoing geopolitical or market story as provisional and to re-run broadened queries before concluding a lull, rather than reporting the absence itself as the finding.
- **Oil-price/geopolitical-risk decoupling.** Brent fell meaningfully across the week even as Hormuz attack tempo and threat ratings stayed elevated — a signal that markets are currently pricing a US political off-ramp (no imminent strike post-midterms) and a Gulf-export recovery more heavily than the physical attack data on the ground. This is worth tracking for whether the oil market's read proves right, or whether it is underpricing continued disruption risk.
- **Opportunistic strategic stake-building around guidance-cut dislocations.** NextDC's post-guidance-cut share slide created an entry point for Goodman Group and Digital Realty to build a meaningful minority stake at a premium to the depressed price; this is a pattern worth watching for in other ASX infrastructure-adjacent names where a guidance cut is attributable to an external delay (power, planning, construction) rather than genuine demand weakness, since such cuts may represent temporary rather than structural value destruction.
